Flowmeters & Semiconductor MFCs: Physics, Equations & Selection Guide

2026-11-14

Selecting a flowmeter starts with the fluid and process duty, not the catalogue. Following the application-first reporting style used by industrial instrumentation leaders such as Honeywell Process Solutions, this guide pairs the governing physics of seven core flow technologies with schematic sketches, selection tables and practical rules of thumb.

2. Traditional Flowmeters: Physics, Equations & Sketches

2.1 Electromagnetic Flowmeter (Magmeter)

Foundation: Faraday’s law of electromagnetic induction. When a conductive liquid passes at mean velocity $v$ through a transverse magnetic field $B$ across a pipe of internal diameter $D$, an electromotive force $E$ is induced perpendicular to both the flow and the field:

$$E = k , B , D , v$$

Volumetric flow rate follows directly from pipe cross-sectional area:

$$Q = \frac{\pi D^2}{4} v = \frac{\pi D}{4 , k , B} E$$

where $k$ is a calibration geometry factor. Because $E$ depends only on $v$, the reading is independent of fluid density, viscosity, temperature and pressure—provided the flow profile is axisymmetric and electrical conductivity exceeds the meter threshold (typically $1\text{–}5\ \mu\text{S/cm}$, per Honeywell VersaFlow Mag).

2.2 Vortex Shedding Flowmeter

Foundation: von Kármán vortex street behind a non-streamlined bluff body of width $d$. Above a critical Reynolds number ($Re \gtrsim 5{,}000\text{–}10{,}000$), vortices shed alternately from each side at frequency $f$, governed by the dimensionless Strouhal number $St$:

$$St = \frac{f , d}{v} \implies v = \frac{f , d}{St}$$

Over the meter’s operating $Re$ plateau, $St$ remains nearly constant ($\approx 0.16\text{–}0.22$ depending on bluff-body geometry), making volumetric flow linear with shedding frequency:

$$Q = A_{\text{eff}} , v = \frac{A_{\text{eff}} , d}{St} f$$

A piezoelectric or capacitive sensor downstream detects the alternating pressure pulses.

2.3 Ultrasonic Flowmeter (Transit-Time & Doppler)

Foundation: Acoustic wave propagation superimposed on fluid velocity (Endress+Hauser).

  1. Transit-Time (Clean Liquids & Gases): Two transducers separated by acoustic path length $L$ at angle $\alpha$ to the pipe axis exchange pulses alternately downstream ($t_{\text{down}}$) and upstream ($t_{\text{up}}$):

$$t_{\text{down}} = \frac{L}{c + v \cos\alpha}, \qquad t_{\text{up}} = \frac{L}{c - v \cos\alpha}$$

Solving for $v$ eliminates the fluid speed of sound $c$ (Soaring Instrument):

$$v = \frac{L}{2 \cos\alpha} \cdot \frac{\Delta t}{t_{\text{up}} , t_{\text{down}}}, \qquad \Delta t = t_{\text{up}} - t_{\text{down}}$$

  1. Doppler (Aerated Liquids & Slurries): Particles or bubbles moving at $v$ scatter an incident wave of frequency $f_0$, producing a Doppler shift $\Delta f$:

$$\Delta f = f_1 - f_0 = \frac{2 f_0 , v \cos\alpha}{c}$$

2.4 Rotameter (Variable Area Flowmeter)

Foundation: Vertical force equilibrium on a float inside a tapered tube (Schwebekörper; DwyerOmega). As flow rises, the float ascends until the upward drag ($F_D$) and buoyancy ($F_B$) balance its weight ($F_G$):

$$F_G = F_B + F_D \implies \rho_f V_f g = \rho V_f g + \frac{1}{2} \rho v_{\text{ann}}^2 C_D A_f$$

Because the annular gap area $A_{\text{ann}}(y)$ widens with float height $y$, the pressure drop across the float stays nearly constant while velocity in the annulus scales as:

$$Q = C_d , A_{\text{ann}}(y) \sqrt{\frac{2 , g , V_f , (\rho_f - \rho)}{\rho , A_f}}$$

where $\rho_f, V_f, A_f$ are the float density, volume and frontal area, and $\rho$ is fluid density.

2.5 Turbine Flowmeter

Foundation: Angular momentum transfer from an axial fluid stream to a low-inertia bladed rotor (Webtec). Neglecting bearing friction and fluid drag, blade angular velocity $\omega$ is directly proportional to mean axial velocity $v$:

$$\omega = \frac{v \tan\beta}{r_{\text{eff}}}$$

where $\beta$ is the blade pitch angle and $r_{\text{eff}}$ is the effective pitch radius. A magnetic or RF pickoff coil counts blade passages at frequency $f$, giving volumetric flow via the meter’s calibrated K-factor (pulses per unit volume):

$$Q = \frac{f}{K}$$

At low flow rates, viscous drag and bearing friction cause $K$ to roll off non-linearly, setting the lower turndown limit.

2.6 Coriolis Mass Flowmeter

Foundation: Coriolis acceleration in a vibrating flow tube. A drive coil oscillates a U-shaped or straight tube at its natural resonant frequency $f_r$ ($\vec{\omega}$). Fluid moving at velocity $\vec{v}$ experiences an opposing Coriolis force on the inlet and outlet legs:

$$\vec{F}_c = -2 , \dot{m} , (\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v})$$

Because the forces act in opposite directions on the inlet and outlet limbs, the tube twists slightly during each half-cycle. Two optical or electromagnetic pickoffs measure the resulting phase difference $\Delta \phi$ or time delay $\Delta t$:

$$\dot{m} = K_u , \Delta t = K_u \frac{\Delta \phi}{2 \pi f_r}$$

Simultaneously, the tube’s resonant frequency shifts with the total vibrating mass (tube + fluid), yielding direct fluid density $\rho \propto 1/f_r^2 - C_0$.

3. Semiconductor Mass Flow Controllers (MFCs): What Happens Inside?

Unlike traditional industrial meters that only measure flow and leave regulation to an external PLC and control valve, a semiconductor MFC integrates sensing, closed-loop PID electronics and a fast proportional actuator (piezo or solenoid valve) inside a single ultra-high-purity (UHP) 316L VIM-VAR stainless-steel block (MKS Instruments; HORIBA).

3.1 Thermal MFC (Capillary Bypass & MEMS/CTA)

What happens inside:

  1. Laminar Flow Splitter (Bypass): Rather than heating the entire gas stream, a precision laminar-flow element forces a fixed fraction of gas through a thin heated capillary sensor tube while the bulk passes through parallel bypass channels.
  2. Heat Transfer Physics: Governed by the first law of thermodynamics and King’s Law, gas molecules carry heat downstream from an upstream heater/sensor coil ($T_1$) to a downstream coil ($T_2$). Over the linear capillary operating regime (MKS Instruments):

$$\dot{m} , c_p = a , \frac{P_w}{\Delta T} \quad \text{or} \quad \Delta T \propto \dot{m} , c_p$$

where $P_w$ is heater power, $c_p$ is specific heat capacity at constant pressure and $a$ is a geometry constant.
3. Closed-Loop Actuation: A Wheatstone bridge converts $\Delta T$ (or constant-temperature power $P_w$) into a voltage, which an onboard PID loop compares against the tool setpoint ($0\text{–}5\ \text{V}$, DeviceNet or EtherCAT) to modulate the control valve in $< 0.5\text{–}1\ \text{s}$.

Why it differs from traditional meters:

  • Direct gas-property dependence ($c_p$, thermal conductivity) means switching from $\text{N}_2$ to $\text{CF}_4$ or $\text{SiH}_4$ requires a gas conversion factor or multi-gas calibration.
  • Sensitive to upstream pressure transients (compressibility shifts the bypass split ratio) and mounting orientation (internal natural convection) unless pressure-compensated (MKS Instruments).

3.2 Pressure-Based MFC (PMFC)

What happens inside:
Used increasingly in modern etch and ALD tools where upstream gas-panel pressure fluctuates (Brooks Instrument), a PMFC places a precision critical-flow orifice (or laminar restrictor) downstream of the control valve and measures upstream pressure $P_1$ (and temperature $T_1$, plus downstream $P_2$ if unchoked). Under choked (sonic) conditions ($P_1 / P_2 \ge \left(\frac{\gamma + 1}{2}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}}$):

$$\dot{m} = C_d , A_{\text{orifice}} , P_1 \sqrt{\frac{\gamma , M}{R , T_1} \left(\frac{2}{\gamma + 1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma + 1}{\gamma - 1}}}$$

Because mass flow depends only on $P_1$ and $T_1$, the valve actively regulates $P_1$ to hold $\dot{m}$ immune to downstream vacuum chamber pressure swings.

4. Engineering Rules of Thumb (Honeywell-Style Selection)

  1. Start with fluid phase and conductivity. Conductive liquid ($\ge 5\ \mu\text{S/cm}$)? Default to a magmeter for zero pressure loss and slurry tolerance. Non-conductive liquid, gas or steam? Move to vortex, ultrasonic or Coriolis.
  2. Check whether mass or volume actually drives your process. Chemical reactions, combustion, custody transfer and semiconductor deposition depend on moles/mass, not volume—use Coriolis (liquids/high-pressure gases) or an MFC (low-flow process gases).
  3. Respect turndown ratio at the minimum flow, not full scale. An instrument rated at $\pm 1%$ FS has $\pm 10%$ error at $10:1$ turndown (Environics). Always check uncertainty at your lowest operating setpoint.
  4. Never ignore straight-run and Reynolds-number limits. Vortex, turbine and single-path ultrasonic meters assume a developed velocity profile ($10\text{–}20D$ upstream). Coriolis and magmeters tolerate tighter piping.
  5. In semiconductor gas boxes, match the MFC architecture to upstream stability. Use thermal MFCs for ultra-low flows and clean steady supply lines; choose pressure-based MFCs where shared gas manifolds cause frequent upstream pressure spikes.

The Core Rule

For a linear, time-invariant (LTI) system with zero initial conditions, the Laplace transform replaces derivatives and integrals with powers of $s$:

$$\frac{d^n y}{dt^n} ;\longleftrightarrow; s^n Y(s), \qquad \underbrace{\int\cdots\int}_{n}, y,dt^n ;\longleftrightarrow; \frac{Y(s)}{s^n}$$

The order of a system is the highest derivative acting on the output — equivalently, the degree of the denominator polynomial in $H(s)$, i.e. the number of poles. Integral terms work the same way but in reverse: they add negative powers of $s$, which get cleared into the denominator once you multiply through.

Comprehensive Guide to System-Level Thermocouple Calibration

2026-04-18

1. Principle of Thermocouple Measurement

Thermocouples operate on the Seebeck Effect: when two dissimilar metals are joined at two points and subjected to a temperature gradient, a proportional thermoelectric voltage is generated.

In a practical data acquisition (DAQ) system, the signal path is defined by the following equation:

$$V_{out} = G [V(T_{hot}) - V(T_{cold}) + V_{cjc}]$$

Variable Breakdown:

  • $T_{hot}$ (Measurement Junction): The actual temperature you are trying to measure at the tip of the probe.
  • $T_{cold}$ (Reference Junction): The point where the thermocouple wires connect to the DAQ system (which is usually copper). This connection creates a new, unwanted thermocouple.
  • $V_{cjc}$ (Cold Junction Compensation): Because the DAQ terminals are at ambient room temperature (not absolute zero or 0°C), we must compensate for this offset. The DAQ uses a localized, high-precision sensor (like an RTD or thermistor) to measure the exact temperature of the terminal block, converts that temperature to its equivalent voltage ($V_{cjc}$), and mathematically adds it back into the equation.
  • $G$ (System Gain): The amplification factor applied by the measurement hardware to scale the microvolt-level signals up to readable levels for the ADC.
